San Francisco Crime Data Story

A citywide decline, but not a citywide experience

A short narrative about how citywide crime trends can hide neighborhood concentration.

This page uses San Francisco police incident data from the city’s open data portal. The dataset records reported incidents, which means it reflects what was recorded by police rather than a perfect measure of underlying crime. That distinction matters: a change in incident counts can reflect changes in behavior, reporting, or enforcement.

I focus here on one category of incidents and one simple question: does the citywide trend describe what people experience in every neighborhood? The short answer is no. The total may fall, but the geography can still stay highly uneven.

1. The citywide trend looks simple

The first figure shows the overall yearly pattern for the selected incident category across San Francisco. At first glance, the story looks straightforward: the number of recorded incidents declines over time.

That does not automatically mean the underlying behavior declined by the same amount. Police incident data is shaped by reporting and enforcement as well as by what happens on the ground. To understand who is still most affected, the next step is to look at place.

2. The pattern is not evenly distributed across the city

A map makes the citywide average harder to trust on its own. Even when the total number of incidents falls, the burden may remain concentrated in a smaller set of neighborhoods.

This is the main point of the story. A city average can move in one direction while the lived geography stays uneven. The map does not tell us why that happens by itself. It could reflect differences in activity, foot traffic, land use, policing, or reporting intensity.

3. Comparing neighborhoods shows different local trajectories

The final figure lets the reader compare neighborhood trends directly. This is still the same story, but now the differences are visible over time rather than only in one spatial snapshot.

Taken together, the three figures tell a more careful story than the citywide line alone. The total count may improve, but the geography still matters. That is why summary numbers should be treated as a starting point rather than the whole conclusion.

Conclusion

The main lesson from this data story is that citywide averages can hide neighborhood concentration. Looking only at the total would make the change seem more uniform than it really is. Once the data is broken out spatially, the pattern becomes more uneven.

At the same time, this dataset should be interpreted carefully. Recorded incident data is not a complete measure of harm, and it should not be treated as a direct measure of criminal behavior without considering reporting practices and enforcement. The value of visualization here is not just to make the pattern visible, but to make its limits visible too.
